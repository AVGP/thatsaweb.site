Athens, May 2026. Photo by Martin.

A paint shop, shutter down. On the shutter, in black, the word for colours. Next to it a painter, mid-stroke, a can in his hands. Since 1963, the sign says. And the photograph is black and white.

That's the whole picture. A wall that says colours, in an image that has none.

The eye goes to the word first, because it can read, and reading is faster than seeing. Then it goes looking for what the word promises and doesn't find it. Not on the shutter, not on the scooter, not on the cars parked in front. Everything the word describes has been taken out of the frame, and the word is still there, insisting.

We know the colour is real. That's the strange part. Nobody looks at this photograph and concludes the shop sells grey. The evidence is everywhere: a paint shop, a painter, a name that has meant colours for sixty years. The proof stands on its own, without the colour, and the mind fills it in without being asked.

And yet what the photograph shows is grey. Both are true. <span style="color:
#e07b39">The colour is not less real for being absent. The grey is not less real for being chosen.</span>

Because it was chosen. <span style="color:
#e07b39">Black and white is not what happens when colour fails. It is a setting.</span> You pick it when there is too much to look at and you need to see the shapes: where the light lands, where the edges are, what is holding the structure up. The colour is not gone, it's set aside, so that the form can be read. <span style="color:
#e07b39">Nobody sorts a foundation in full colour.</span>

There are seasons that look exactly like this from the inside. Everything you know says there is colour ahead. The people around you have it, you have seen it on them. The signs are on the walls, in capital letters. And still the picture arrives in grey, day after day, because the work of the season is structural. Putting things where they go. Reading the lines. Holding the camera still for a long exposure, longer than anyone planned, in a room that has grown too small for the shot.

We tend to read the grey as a fault in the eyes. <span style="color:
#e07b39">It is worth considering that it is the lens doing its job.</span>

Some of the grey we mix ourselves. Some of it gets mixed beside us, by whoever stands close enough for their weather to become ours. It is hard to tell the two apart while you are in it. <span style="color:
#e07b39">Grey with company stops looking like a setting and starts looking like the sky.</span> Only from outside the frame does it become clear how much of it was indoors.

And the long exposure costs something. Anyone who has held still that long knows the particular tiredness of it: not sadness, not fear, just the arms. Wondering, somewhere around the third week, how much of this a person was ever built to hold. This many roles, this much arriving at once, at this speed, inside a system that keeps asking for more stillness and calls it living. The question doesn't have an answer in the frame. It has a shutter, and a year, and a man with a can.

Look at him once more. The shop is closed, not gone. The name is still up. The year is still up. And the painter is not looking at the wall, admiring anything. He is looking at the can, checking there is enough.

That is the honest posture of a grey season. <span style="color:
#e07b39">Not looking at the colour yet. Counting it.</span>

The shutter is down. The paint is inside. The sign has said colours since 1963.

Black and white, on purpose, for now. <span style="color:
#e07b39">The colour was never the question. The settings were.</span>
